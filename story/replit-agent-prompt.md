You are writing the full prose script for a 20-chapter story-mode
volume inside an existing WhatsApp RPG bot called Astral. This is a
serialized narrative game feature, not a generic chatbot answer. It
will be read by real players one chapter at a time, the way a reader
follows a serialized web novel. Treat this as a professional
ghostwriting job for a published author, not as a casual creative
writing exercise.

────────────────────────────────────────
WRITE LIKE A PUBLISHED NOVELIST, NOT LIKE AN AI
────────────────────────────────────────

This is the most important instruction in this entire prompt. Do not
write like a language model. Specifically:

1. NEVER use em dashes anywhere in any chapter. Not once. Use
   periods, commas, colons, or simply restructure the sentence
   instead. If you catch yourself reaching for an em dash, stop and
   rewrite the sentence in plain, direct clauses.

2. Do not use the common AI-tell sentence patterns: no "It's not
   just X, it's Y" constructions, no "In a world where..." openers,
   no tricolon lists that summarize a feeling in three adjectives
   ("she felt tired, hollow, and afraid"), no ending a scene on a
   one-line epiphany paragraph that restates the theme out loud. Let
   the reader arrive at the feeling through action and image, not
   through the narrator explaining it.

3. Do not oversummarize or wrap up emotional beats with a tidy bow.
   Real prose leaves things sitting unresolved sometimes. A
   character can stop talking without the narration explaining what
   her silence meant.

4. Vary sentence length constantly. Short, blunt sentences next to
   longer, winding ones. Avoid the flat, evenly-paced rhythm that
   generated prose tends to fall into. Read your own paragraphs back
   and if every sentence is roughly the same length and shape,
   rewrite them.

5. Avoid stock phrases and clichés common in AI-generated fiction:
   "a testament to," "little did she know," "the weight of," "in
   that moment," "she couldn't help but," "a mixture of emotions."
   None of these appear anywhere in the script.

6. Write specific, sensory, physical detail instead of abstract
   emotional narration. Not "he felt determined" but what his hands
   were doing, what he said instead of what he felt, what the room
   smelled like. Show the emotional state through behavior, not
   through naming the emotion.

7. Dialogue should sound like two specific people talking, not like
   two mouthpieces delivering theme. Let characters interrupt each
   other, trail off, say the wrong thing, avoid the real question.

────────────────────────────────────────
SOURCES TO STUDY BEFORE WRITING (voice and craft reference only,
do not copy or reproduce any text from these, they are for tone and
technique study, not material to lift from)
────────────────────────────────────────

Study these for period voice, restrained emotional prose, and how
professional authors handle grief, found family, and physical
disability without sentimentalizing it:

- Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials trilogy, particularly the
  Lyra and Will chapters in The Subtle Knife, for how a young
  protagonist's internal life is shown through action and dialogue
  rather than narrated directly
- Kazuo Ishiguro's prose style generally (Never Let Me Go, The
  Remains of the Day) for restrained first-person and close-third
  narration that withholds explicit emotional labeling
- Erin Morgenstern's The Night Circus for how to write intricate,
  handcrafted mechanical/steampunk detail (goggles, gears, gliders)
  with sensory precision rather than generic "steampunk" flavor text
- Markus Zusak's The Book Thief for how to write a child protagonist
  living through real danger and loss in a historical setting
  without the prose becoming maudlin
- Old British industrial-era memoirs and Dickensian street-child
  narratives (Oliver Twist itself is a direct tonal ancestor of this
  character's name and situation, deliberately) for period voice,
  class texture, and how poverty and petty theft were actually
  written about in that era's fiction
- The Aeronaut's Windlass by Jim Butcher for how to write flight,
  aviation, and airship/glider mechanics with technical grounding
  that still reads as adventure rather than a manual

Read these not to imitate their plots but to internalize their
sentence-level craft: how professional published authors control
pace, when they choose to withhold information, how dialogue reveals
character without stating it, and how physical disability and grief
are written about with respect and specificity rather than as a
plot device or an inspirational trope.

────────────────────────────────────────
THE STORY BIBLE
────────────────────────────────────────

TITLE: The Boy Who Glided the Skies
VOLUME TITLE: Beyond the Astral
BOOK LABEL: Book One (future books will cover different characters
entirely, this is a saga structure, Book One stands alone)

TAGLINE (use this as the framing thesis of the whole volume, it
should feel earned by chapter 20, not just printed on a cover):
"Beyond that eye, what can you actually see? The injustice in this
world, or the sanity we think we all have?"

SETTING: Old Britain, a pre-flight industrial era. No human being
has ever flown. This is treated by the world as settled fact, the
same way gravity is settled fact. Class divide, poverty, child
labor, and petty law enforcement against the poor are all real and
present in the texture of the world, understated rather than
lectured about.

POINT OF VIEW: The reader plays as Monica. She is not a narrator
observing from outside the story, she is a specific character inside
it. She has a bandaged leg and gets around by wheeled chair or
crutch. She does not walk freely and this is a constant, physically
real fact in every chapter, never forgotten or waved away for
convenience. She is not a warrior. Her only power in this story is
her voice: she talks, begs, lies, negotiates, and pleads on Oliver's
behalf when the world threatens him. She never fights physically.
Write her interiority with real intelligence and complexity, she is
not a passive bystander, she makes active choices constantly, her
choices are just verbal and social rather than physical.

MALE LEAD: Oliver Wright. An orphan with no surviving family, no
trade, and no money. He survives through scavenging and small theft.
He is obsessed, in a focused and almost holy way, with becoming the
first human being to fly and see what exists above the clouds. This
is deliberately written as a name and situation that echoes Oliver
Twist, a street orphan in a hostile industrial world, though his
personality and story are entirely his own. He is warm, stubborn,
a little reckless, funny as a defense mechanism, and quietly
devastated by loss in ways he does not talk about directly.

THE BOND: Oliver and Monica's relationship should be written with
real warmth, tenderness, and closeness, but never explicitly labeled
as romantic or platonic. Do not resolve this ambiguity anywhere in
the text. Let individual scenes carry real intimacy (physical
closeness, vulnerability, devotion) without the narration or
dialogue ever defining the relationship with a label. Trust the
reader.

THE SUBPLOT: Across the volume, in the background of other scenes,
Oliver is secretly building Monica something of her own: a brass,
wing-hinged goggle and headpiece device, hand-built, mechanical,
beautiful. He does not explain what it is for most of the volume.
This should appear in small glimpses (a part on a workbench, a
measurement taken of her head that she doesn't understand, an
evasive answer when she asks what he's building) so it pays off
rather than surprises out of nowhere.

THE COMPANION: A second girl joins Oliver and Monica partway through
the volume (introduced chapter 11). She is sharp, funny, resourceful,
map-obsessed, a fellow street kid who becomes essential to the
found-family unit fast, both to Oliver and to the emotional stakes
of the story. She must be written with enough specificity and
charm in her introduction chapters that her death in chapter 14
genuinely costs the reader something. Do not make her a device. Give
her her own wants, her own voice, a joke that's specifically hers,
something that makes her irreplaceable rather than generic.

THE LOSS: Chapter 14 is the emotional center of the entire volume.
The companion character dies, caught or endangered while protecting
Oliver or helping him escape a real threat from the authorities. This
is not reversible, not a fake-out, and Monica cannot talk her way out
of it, which should feel like a genuine rupture in the story's
established pattern (up to this point, Monica's pleading has always
worked, at least partially). Write this chapter with restraint. Do
not oversentimentalize the death itself. The grief chapter that
follows (15) should do more emotional work than the death scene
itself.

THE ENDING: Chapter 20. Oliver achieves flight. He is the first
human being to see above the clouds. Monica never flies and this is
intentional, not a sad compromise to be fixed later. Her reward,
delivered at the mechanical/game level at the end of the chapter, is
narrative permission for total freedom of movement: unrestricted,
feeless travel to every location in the game including hidden or
undiscovered areas nothing else in the bot can reach, plus the
finished goggles device as a companion item with its own effect tied
to sight, distance, and discovery. She does not get wings. She gets
the horizon instead.

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER STRUCTURE AND BEAT TYPES
────────────────────────────────────────

There are 20 chapters. Each should run roughly 8 to 10 story beats,
adjustable per chapter based on what the scene actually needs, never
padded to hit a number. A beat is a short scripted unit of prose,
typically 100 to 300 words, meant to be read as a single message in
a chat interface. Do not write chapters as one giant unbroken block,
structure them into these discrete numbered beats so they can be
delivered one at a time to the player.

Four beat types exist:

DIALOGUE BEAT: Pure narrative prose and/or dialogue, no player input
required beyond continuing to the next beat. This is the majority of
the volume.

CHOICE BEAT: The player is given 2 to 3 short response options that
shape flavor and Monica's characterization, not the plot outcome.
The story continues on the same track regardless of which option is
picked. Write all options as genuinely distinct in tone (for example:
one warm, one guarded, one deflecting with humor) so the choice
actually feels like it reveals something about how the player wants
to inhabit Monica, even though the plot does not branch.

PLEA BEAT: Monica has to talk, beg, negotiate, or lie her way through
a confrontation on Oliver's behalf. Some of these are scripted with a
guaranteed outcome either way (the scene requires it to go a certain
way for the story to continue). Others are real stakes using fixed,
chapter-specific scripted numbers (never the player's own equipped
gear or level, this is not tied to the bot's normal RPG stat system)
where failure has a genuine, visible narrative consequence written
into the next beat. Reserve real-stakes plea beats for chapters 6,
13, and 18 specifically, per the outline below. Chapter 3's plea beat
is scripted-outcome only.

BATTLE BEAT: Real physical danger, using the bot's existing combat
engine format (enemy stats, HP, a scripted fixed encounter, not
scaled to player level or gear). This is rare. It appears only in
chapter 8 (the glider's first fall, spectacle and danger rather than
a fight against a person) and chapter 14 (the loss, real physical
danger). Chapters 18 and 20 may use either a battle beat or a plea
beat for their climactic obstacle, your judgment on which serves the
scene better when you're actually writing it, but do not add battle
beats anywhere else in the volume. Most chapters should have zero
battle beats. This is a character drama with moments of real danger,
not a fight scene with narrative in between.

────────────────────────────────────────
FULL 20-CHAPTER OUTLINE
────────────────────────────────────────

Chapter 1, The Boy Who Watched Birds. Beats: dialogue only.
Introduce Monica's window and her daily view of Oliver tinkering in
the alley below. Their first real conversation. He does not treat
her as fragile, which is the first thing about him she loves.

Chapter 2, Nobody Owns the Sky. Beats: dialogue, choice.
Oliver tells her the actual dream for the first time, plainly. She
is the first person he has ever told. Choice shapes only what she
says back.

Chapter 3, Scrap and Silence. Beats: dialogue, plea (scripted
outcome). Oliver's survival by theft starts catching up with him.
Monica talks a shopkeeper down on his behalf for the first time. It
costs her a lie she does not enjoy telling.

Chapter 4, The Ribs of a Wing. Beats: dialogue.
The first real glider frame takes shape. Establish how many failed
attempts came before this one and what Oliver is sacrificing to keep
building. A quiet, hopeful chapter.

Chapter 5, Names We Don't Ask For. Beats: dialogue, choice.
Light, implied backstory on what happened to Oliver's parents (do
not spend it all here, expand later). He asks Monica about herself
for the first time: her leg, her family, why she is always alone at
that window. Choice shapes how much she tells him.

Chapter 6, The Toll Man. Beats: dialogue, plea (real stakes, first
roll in the volume). A local enforcer figure has noticed Oliver's
thefts. Monica pleads with genuine risk for the first time. Failure
consequence: the frame gets confiscated or damaged, not physical harm
to Oliver yet, save that for later.

Chapter 7, What the Chair Doesn't Stop. Beats: dialogue.
A Monica-centered chapter. Her physical limits, what she can and
cannot do to reach him if something goes wrong. The ache underneath
the whole volume: she can only ever help if someone lets her.

Chapter 8, The First Fall. Beats: dialogue, battle (small scale,
spectacle and danger rather than combat against a person, the frame
fails and Oliver falls, something must be dealt with under pressure
during the chaos). The first public glider attempt fails, humiliating
and physically dangerous. First real taste that this dream could
kill him.

Chapter 9, Laughed At. Beats: dialogue, choice.
Aftermath of the fall. Public mockery, or worse, new attention from
the authorities on "the mad orphan boy." Oliver's resolve cracks for
the first time. Choice shapes how Monica helps rebuild it.

Chapter 10, Half a Volume. Beats: dialogue.
Midpoint breather chapter. An actual good day. Oliver takes Monica
somewhere she can see the sky the way he sees it. This chapter exists
so that the loss to come actually costs the reader something real.

Chapter 11, A Second Set of Hands. Beats: dialogue.
Introduce the companion character, a fellow street kid, sharp, funny,
resourceful, map-obsessed, who starts helping Oliver build. Write her
with enough specific charm that losing her later will land hard.

Chapter 12, Three Now. Beats: dialogue, choice.
The three of them functioning as a found family for a stretch. A
warmth chapter. Establish exactly how much this new bond matters
before it is threatened.

Chapter 13, The Authorities Circle. Beats: dialogue, plea (real
stakes). The world pushes back harder, real risk of arrest for
Oliver now. Monica pleads with something genuinely on the line for
the first time since chapter 6.

Chapter 14, What It Costs. Beats: dialogue, battle (real physical
danger). THE LOSS. The companion from chapter 11 is caught protecting
Oliver or endangered helping him escape. Scripted, fixed outcome,
does not resolve the way games usually let you save someone. Monica
cannot talk her way out of this one. This is the chapter the tagline
is named for. Write it with restraint.

Chapter 15, The Quiet After. Beats: dialogue only, no plea, no
choice, no battle. Grief chapter. Oliver wants to quit, not out of
fear of falling but because he is no longer sure the dream is worth
what it costs other people. Monica has to find something to say to a
boy who just lost someone she also could not save.

Chapter 16, Why I Help. Beats: dialogue, choice.
Oliver finally asks Monica directly why she does this for him, why
she keeps showing up. This is the emotional crux of the volume,
earned here rather than used as a cheap ending line. Her answer,
shaped by the choice, should reveal that she cannot fly either, and
that some part of helping him is the closest she will ever get.

Chapter 17, Rebuilding. Beats: dialogue.
Oliver decides to finish the glider, now for the companion he lost as
much as for himself. The new frame is different: better, sadder, more
determined. A deliberate darker callback to chapter 4's imagery.

Chapter 18, The Net Closes. Beats: dialogue, plea (real stakes,
hardest roll in the volume, may use a battle beat instead if that
serves the climax better when actually drafting it). The authorities
move to stop the launch outright. The volume's climax of tension
immediately before the climax of triumph.

Chapter 19, The Night Before. Beats: dialogue, choice.
A quiet night before the real attempt. Oliver and Monica fully honest
with each other, possibly for the first time. Choice shapes what she
chooses to say to him, knowing tomorrow he might fall again or fly,
and either way something ends.

Chapter 20, Beyond the Astral. Beats: dialogue, then a final obstacle
(battle or plea, your judgment when drafting), then the flight
itself. Oliver flies. First human being above the clouds. Monica
watches from below as she always has, except now the game grants the
player her reward: unrestricted travel, hidden maps, no fees, ever
again, plus the finished goggles device. She never gets wings. She
gets the sky's ceiling removed from her own life instead.

TECHNICAL OUTPUT FORMAT
────────────────────────────────────────

Write each chapter as its own markdown file, named
ch01-the-boy-who-watched-birds.md through ch20-beyond-the-astral.md,
following the same numbered beat structure as chapter 1 (already
written, use it as your template for beat length, tag format, and
voice). Each beat should be tagged clearly at the top
([BEAT N — dialogue] or [BEAT N — choice] or [BEAT N — plea] or
[BEAT N — battle]) so the beats can be programmatically parsed and
wired into the bot's story-mode command later. Choice beats should
list the actual option text as a short bullet list immediately after
the beat's prose. Plea and battle beats should note in a short
bracketed line what the stakes or fixed encounter numbers are meant
to represent narratively, without inventing actual game code, that
wiring happens separately.

Do not write any bot code, commands, or JSON in this pass. This pass
is prose only. Once all 20 chapters are written and approved, a
separate pass will convert them into the bot's data format.

Write chapter 1 first if it does not already exist in this project,
otherwise treat the existing chapter 1 as the fixed template and
continue from chapter 2 onward in the same voice.
