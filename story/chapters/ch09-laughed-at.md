# Chapter 9: Laughed At

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

By morning, the quarry has a story.

By noon, it has six.

The glider flew. The glider did not fly. Oliver fell because he was drunk. Oliver fell because he was cursed. A demon lives in the frame. The girl in the chair pulled the rope and sent him into the ground.

The last version travels fastest.

A newspaper boy shouts it outside the bakery. “Cripple girl crashes orphan boy! Read all about the new danger in the skies!”

Oliver throws a washer at him. It misses.

“You have made us famous,” you say.

“I was aiming for obscure.”

He has a bandage across his temple. His arm is in a sling made from the butcher's apron. He works one-handed on the broken cradle.

“People are saying I crashed you,” you add.

“People say many things.”

“They say I should be arrested.”

“For what?”

“Poor judgment.”

He looks at the broken wing. “That charge may stand.”

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

The machine yard sends a messenger. He is not the Toll Man. He is a thin clerk with spectacles and a sealed letter.

“Oliver Wright,” he says.

“He is out,” you reply.

Oliver sits three feet behind you with his head down.

The clerk looks at him.

“He is out of the conversation,” you say.

The clerk opens the letter. “The yard requires payment for materials recovered from the quarry.”

“Recovered from the quarry?”

“The glider broke on land belonging to Mr. Bell.”

“Did Mr. Bell buy the sky?”

The clerk blinks.

Oliver whispers, “Do not start.”

“I have started.”

The clerk reads on. “Failure to settle will result in seizure of property.”

You glance at the broken frame.

“What property?”

The clerk's eyes move to Oliver's coat. “Anything of value.”

Oliver laughs once. It is the wrong sound.

The clerk leaves the letter on the bench and goes.

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

Oliver removes the sling and tries to lift the cradle.

“You are not helping,” you say.

“I am testing the brace.”

“With your injured arm.”

“It is a brace test.”

He lifts. Pain crosses his face. He lowers the wood.

“The brace is sound,” he says.

“Your arm is not.”

“My arm is attached.”

“That is a low standard.”

He stares at the frame. A strip of canvas still carries the fish-shaped patch. The rest of the wing has been stripped down to ribs and broken thread.

“The right spar was too light,” he says.

“The right spar was attached to a flying machine that fell down a hill.”

“Both can be true.”

“Must you take notes?”

“If I do not, the hill wins.”

He reaches for his black notebook. The cover is muddy from the quarry. Several pages have torn loose.

You pick them up before the floor can take them.

## [BEAT 4 — choice]

Oliver cannot decide whether to rebuild. He keeps arranging the broken pieces, then putting them back in the same pile. At last he asks, “What do I do now?”

You can tell him:

- “Start with the smallest part. It will give you somewhere to put your hands.”
- “Stop before the city takes more from you.”
- “Make them laugh again, then make them sorry.”

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

He listens to your answer. He does not agree at once. That is how you know it reached him.

The afternoon passes without work. Oliver sits on the bench with the black notebook open, his pencil balanced between two fingers. He draws nothing.

At the end of the lane, the factory boys appear. The one with the cut palm is not among them.

“There he is,” one calls. “The sky king.”

Another flaps his arms.

Oliver keeps looking at the empty page.

You turn your chair toward the doorway.

“Do not,” he says.

“I have not done anything.”

“You are about to.”

The boys come closer. One picks up the damaged fish patch.

“Is this your flag?”

“Put it down,” Oliver says.

The boy tosses it at his chest.

You raise your voice. “If you are stealing, take something worth stealing.”

The boys look at you.

“That cloth is worth less than the boots you wore here.”

They laugh. The laughter is not kind, but it moves their attention away from Oliver.

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

The tallest boy steps toward you. “You think you are clever.”

“No. I think you are loud.”

“You cannot even get out of that chair.”

“And yet you came inside to hear me.”

The boy's mouth opens. Nothing comes out.

Oliver has risen behind you. His injured arm hangs at his side. He looks smaller without the frame around him, but the boy sees him standing and takes one step backward.

“Come on,” another boy says.

They leave the fish patch on the floor.

Oliver closes the door.

“You did not have to say that,” he says.

“That phrase has become your favorite.”

“They could have hurt you.”

“They could have tried.”

“You cannot fight.”

“I did not fight.”

He picks up the patch and smooths it across his knee.

“You made them leave.”

“They were bored.”

“You made them bored.”

“A gift.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

That night, Oliver removes the right spar from the broken frame. The wood has split along the grain.

“It was too light,” he says.

“You said.”

“It needed weight.”

“You said it needed to be light.”

“It needed to be light in a different direction.”

You watch him scrape the split clean. He works slowly, as if the wood might speak if he gives it time.

“What if they are right?” you ask.

He does not look up.

“About what?”

“The sky.”

“The sky is above us.”

“You know what I mean.”

He sets down the knife. “No. I know what they said.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is when people are laughing.”

He folds the fish patch and places it in his pocket.

“The first frame was not meant to fly far,” he says.

“How far was it meant to fly?”

“Far enough to prove the next one should exist.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

The next morning, the repaired bearing on your chair begins to squeal. Oliver hears it from the bench.

He looks at the wheel, then at you.

“Do not say it.”

“I need oil.”

“You said it.”

He takes a small tin from the shelf. The label has been scraped away. You hold the chair steady while he applies a drop to the bearing.

The squeal fades.

“There,” he says.

“The chair is famous now.”

“It has a reputation.”

He returns to the frame. The new spar is darker wood, heavier, and curved by steam. He sets it beside the broken one.

“That is the smallest part?” you ask.

“It is the first.”

Outside, someone shouts the newspaper story again. Oliver does not throw a washer this time.

He puts the pencil to paper.

The next drawing takes up the whole page.
