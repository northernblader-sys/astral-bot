# Chapter 7: What the Chair Doesn't Stop

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

The day after the Toll Man leaves, Oliver tries to improve your chair.

You find him beneath it with a screwdriver, one knee pressed into the floor and his cheek against the axle.

“Get out,” you say.

“I am inspecting.”

“You are trespassing.”

“The wheel is making a sound.”

“It has always made that sound.”

“The sound has changed.”

“You have known it for one day.”

“That is long enough to notice a change.”

You grip the armrests and push backward. The chair rolls away. Oliver sits up and bangs his head against the table.

“You did that on purpose.”

“I did it with purpose.”

He rubs his forehead. “The left bearing is worn.”

“So am I. You are not taking me apart to find out where.”

“I was not going to.”

“You were holding a wrench.”

“For the wheel.”

“That is what you say about everything.”

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

You take the chair out of the washhouse and along the wall behind the bakery. Oliver follows with the repaired glider frame balanced on his shoulder.

The frame catches every breeze. Its canvas lifts and drops. People turn to watch. A woman carrying a basket laughs under her breath. A boy throws a pebble that lands near Oliver's boot.

“You should cover it,” you say.

“It needs air.”

“It needs fewer witnesses.”

He angles the frame so it shields you from the road. The wind presses the canvas against his face.

“That is not better,” you say.

“I have become a sail.”

“A badly made one.”

He pivots. The frame bumps a drainpipe and sends a shower of rust down his back.

You laugh.

“I am pleased,” he says. “My suffering has improved your morning.”

The route ends at a shallow set of steps. There are five. You stop at the bottom.

Oliver sets the frame down.

“We can go around,” he says.

“The road is blocked by the coal cart.”

“Then we move the coal cart.”

“You move it.”

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

The coal cart does not move. Oliver strains against the wheel until the muscles in his neck stand out. The horse turns its head and looks at him with open contempt.

“The horse dislikes you,” you say.

“The horse has no imagination.”

“It has excellent judgment.”

He tries the cart again. A porter shouts from the warehouse.

“Leave it alone, boy.”

Oliver steps away. You look at the five steps. The nearest ramp is two streets over. The rain has left a slick black line on each stone.

“I can carry the frame up,” Oliver says.

“And me?”

He looks at the steps. “I can carry you.”

“No.”

“I was going to say I can find another route.”

“You were not.”

“I thought about it.”

“Thinking is not asking.”

He sits on the bottom step, defeated by the cart, the frame, and your temper.

You turn the chair toward the warehouse. A side gate stands open beside the wall. It is narrow, but the wheels fit.

“There,” you say.

Oliver lifts the frame. “You saw that?”

“I have eyes.”

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

The gate leads to a paved yard behind the warehouse. No steps. No coal cart. A narrow lane opens on the other side.

Oliver looks back at the stairs.

“Most people would have tried those,” he says.

“Most people can.”

He starts to answer, then stops.

You wheel through the yard. The stones are uneven, and the chair's repaired wheel complains at each seam. You cannot push fast enough to keep pace with him. Oliver walks backward, carrying the frame and watching your wheels.

“Turn around,” you say.

“You might fall.”

“If I fall, I will tell you.”

“You might not have time.”

“I have time to shout.”

He turns. The frame rises over his head like a pale, unfinished wing.

At the lane, he lowers it. “I am sorry.”

“For what?”

“The steps.”

“You did not build them.”

“No.”

“Then stop apologizing for every object that fails to fit me.”

He looks down at the frame.

“I can build a ramp,” he says.

“For the city?”

“For the places we need.”

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

The phrase stays with you until afternoon. The places we need.

Your room is full of places other people have chosen. The bed is against the wall because it leaves space for the landlady to carry in coal. The table is low because it belonged to a child. The window is high because the room was made for someone who stood.

At the washhouse, Oliver lays two boards over the threshold.

“A ramp,” he announces.

The boards bow beneath your chair. One nail rises like a tooth.

“This is a trap.”

“It is a first version.”

“The first version is a trap.”

“You are difficult to please.”

“I am difficult to kill.”

He adds a crosspiece. You test it with one wheel. The board shifts.

Oliver catches the frame behind you.

You freeze.

He says, “I have you.”

“You have the chair.”

“I know.”

You reverse onto solid ground. The nail scrapes the tire.

Oliver removes the boards without argument.

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

“What do you do when something goes wrong?” he asks.

“I complain.”

“After that?”

“I find the part of it that can be changed.”

“And if nothing can?”

“I change the plan.”

He studies your hands. “That is why you help me.”

“I help you because you arrive at my window with stolen tea.”

“That is one reason.”

“You are trying to make me answer.”

“You are trying not to.”

You look toward the glider. Its wing rests against the wall. The canvas has dried stiff from the morning wind.

“You can reach things I cannot,” you say. “You can climb walls. You can run when the constables come. You can take a piece of the world and carry it back.”

Oliver's face changes.

“That is not all I do,” he says.

“It is enough.”

“Is it?”

You turn the chair toward the window. “Make the wing work.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

He does. The next hours pass in small repairs. He replaces the nail in the ramp with a bolt. He sands the splintered board. He changes the angle until your wheels cross without slipping.

You test it six times.

On the seventh, you stop halfway and look down at him.

“Do not say anything.”

“I was going to say it works.”

“That is saying something.”

“It works.”

You cross the threshold. The chair rolls cleanly into the washhouse.

Oliver raises both hands as if you have landed from a great height.

“No applause,” you say.

“It was an important journey.”

“It was four feet.”

“All journeys are measured by difficulty.”

“Who taught you that?”

“A horse.”

You glance at him.

“The horse was very wise,” he says.

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

That evening, you sit outside the washhouse while Oliver works on the glider. Your crutch rests across the chair arms, ready for the short stand you use when the seat has become unbearable.

You stand once. The muscles above your knee tremble. The crutch slips against the stone. Oliver looks over.

“Do not,” you say.

He turns back to the frame.

You lower yourself into the chair. The movement takes longer than standing. When you are seated, your bandage has come loose. You retie it yourself.

The wing creaks under Oliver's hands.

“What if it does not stop anything?” he asks.

“What?”

“The chair. The ramp. The work.”

You watch a cloud pass behind the chimneys. “Then we learn what it does stop.”

Oliver threads the needle through the canvas. The point emerges bright, then disappears again.

You cannot reach him if he falls from a roof. You cannot chase him through the machine yard. You cannot fight the men who decide where poor children may stand.

You can make them answer you.

For tonight, that is enough.
