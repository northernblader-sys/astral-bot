# Chapter 4: The Ribs of a Wing

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

Oliver returns at sunrise with a sack of willow strips and a bruise beneath one eye.

“You look dreadful,” you tell him.

“Thank you.”

“It was not praise.”

“I accepted it in the spirit given.”

He has spent the night in the washhouse. You can tell by the damp black along his cuffs and the way he keeps one shoulder stiff. When he lifts the sack, something inside knocks against the brick.

“What happened to your face?”

“A disagreement with a wall.”

“Who won?”

“The wall has a better solicitor.”

He empties the sack onto a folded sheet. The willow strips lie in pale, flexible rows. Beside them he places the carved rib from the first frame, then another, then another. Each one is shaped to the same curve.

You count twelve.

“You made all those?”

“Not all this morning.”

“I was about to say.”

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

He carries the pieces into the washhouse while you follow the smoother path around the back. The chair's repaired wheel behaves well until a stone turns beneath it. Oliver reaches for the handle.

“May I?”

“If you ask again, I may decide you are ill.”

He takes the chair over the stone. His grip is light. He does not steer you away from the broken floor. He finds a route through it.

Inside, the frame has grown. Two long spars run from a central joint, with ribs curving outward like fingers spread beneath a sheet.

“It needs a second side,” Oliver says.

“Most birds have two.”

“Some insects have four.”

“You are not building an insect.”

“Not with that attitude.”

The ribs are tied to the spars with linen thread coated in glue. Oliver has drawn measurements on the floor in chalk.

“Where did you learn this?”

He shrugs. “Watching.”

“Watching what?”

“Anything that stays together.”

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

The first attempt hangs from the rafters by two ropes. Oliver climbs a ladder with a strip of canvas between his teeth. You hold the ladder's foot because there is no one else to do it.

“If you fall,” you say, “I will not catch you.”

“You would catch me with your voice.”

“My voice has poor reach.”

“You have not used it properly.”

He spreads the canvas over the ribs. The cloth is patched from flour sacks, an old sail, and what may once have been a green curtain. When the wind enters through the broken window, the material trembles. The ribs flex. The wing gives a dry, beautiful creak.

Oliver looks down.

“There,” he says.

“It has not flown.”

“It has remembered how.”

He climbs down. On the floor, the shadow of the frame lies across both of you. The shape reaches farther than the room.

You touch one of the knots. It is rough against your fingertip.

“What will you sit in?” you ask.

Oliver points to a narrow wooden cradle not yet attached to the frame.

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

He shows you the cradle as though it is an ordinary chair. A seat cut from a packing case. Two foot braces. A belt made from a butcher's apron.

“It will hold one person,” he says.

“Comfortably?”

“No.”

“Safely?”

“That is the next question.”

The cradle has a brass hinge at either side. You recognize one piece from the shelf.

“You stole those from the machine yard.”

“Borrowed.”

“The yard sent a man after you.”

“He was not from the yard.”

“He wore its coat.”

“People wear many coats.”

You glance at the bruise.

Oliver adjusts the belt. “The first fourteen failed.”

“You said there were fourteen versions.”

“There were fifteen, if we count the one that never left the drawing.”

“What failed?”

“The first collapsed. The second spun. The third went into a pond. The fourth caught fire because I had not understood oil. The fifth was eaten by a dog.”

“Eaten?”

“The canvas.”

He continues until you raise a hand.

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

“What happened to the others?” you ask.

Oliver rubs a dark mark from the cradle with his thumb. “They taught me things.”

“That was not the question.”

“It is the answer I have.”

You wheel to the far wall, where pieces of broken frames lean beneath a shelf. A bent spar. Three charred ribs. A small wheel with one spoke missing. The failures occupy more space than the new machine.

“You kept them.”

“Parts can be reused.”

“Even the burnt ones?”

“Especially those. They know what not to do.”

He crouches beside the pile and lifts a feather caught between two slats. It is grey at the tip and white near the stem.

“Where did you get that?”

“A rook.”

“Did you hurt it?”

“No. It left.”

He lays the feather on the floor with care.

“You cannot make a bird out of scraps,” you say.

“I am not trying to.”

The answer is so plain that you believe him.

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

The day turns bright and cold. You work in the washhouse while Oliver binds each rib. He asks for the thread, the knife, the glue. You pass them from the tray mounted beside your chair.

At midday, you notice a small brass plate on the workbench. It has been filed into a shallow curve. Two tiny hinges sit along one edge.

“What is that?”

Oliver covers it with his palm. “A part.”

“Of the wing?”

“Not exactly.”

“Of the cradle?”

“Not exactly.”

“Of the spoon?”

He looks at you. “That would be a remarkable spoon.”

You hold out your hand.

“No.”

“You are hiding it badly.”

“I am hiding it perfectly. You have merely noticed.”

He slides the plate into a wooden box and locks the box with a bent nail.

“If it is a secret,” you say, “it is a poor one.”

“Most good secrets begin that way.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

When the last rib is tied, Oliver stands back. The wing fills the washhouse. The central spar reaches above his head. Canvas hangs loose at the trailing edge, and one corner has a stitched patch shaped like a fish.

He takes a running step. The wing shivers in the draught.

“Do not run indoors,” you say.

He runs again.

The frame lifts half an inch from its ropes. The sound is not a flap. It is a deep breath made of cloth and timber.

Oliver stops. His eyes go to the ceiling.

You laugh before you mean to.

He turns. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“You laughed.”

“The wing did not.”

“It will.”

He reaches for the leading spar. His bruised cheek pulls when he smiles.

For the first time, the machine does not look like a pile of stolen objects. It looks like something with an argument to make.

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

The rain begins after sunset. Water taps through the broken roof. Oliver spreads oilcloth over the frame and checks every tie twice.

“You should sleep,” you say.

“You should go upstairs.”

“I am upstairs.”

“Then sleep here.”

He has made a narrow place beside the workbench where your chair can turn. You park there while he sits on the floor with his back against the cradle.

The rain fills the silence.

“When it works,” you say, “will you tell people your name?”

“Everyone knows my name.”

“They know what to call you when they want you gone.”

He watches the dark window. “Then I will give them something else to call me.”

“What?”

“I have not decided.”

You look at the wing under its oilcloth. A pale rib presses against the cover like a bone beneath skin.

“Make sure it is not foolish.”

“That removes several excellent options.”

He rests his head against the cradle. You do not tell him that the bruise is swelling.
