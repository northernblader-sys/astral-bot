# Chapter 2: Nobody Owns the Sky

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

Oliver's proper invention is a wooden box with one side missing. It sits on the pavement beneath your window, wrapped in a blanket that has once been red. The blanket is tied with string. One corner has been patched with a man's necktie.

“You could have brought it through the door,” you call.

“The door has opinions.”

“The door is a door.”

“It knows I am poor.”

He unties the bundle. Inside, a set of thin slats crosses a frame no wider than his shoulders. The whole thing resembles the skeleton of a door designed by someone who had only heard doors described.

You lean closer, keeping both hands on the window ledge. “What is it?”

Oliver looks up at you. The morning wind has put soot in his hair.

“A question,” he says.

“It looks like firewood.”

“That is what I want people to think.”

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

He carries the frame into the alley and places it beside the rain barrel. Every joint has been wrapped in cloth. Copper wire binds two of the slats. The rest are held by nails of different sizes.

“How many times have you built it?” you ask.

“This version?”

“The question.”

Oliver squints at the frame. “Fourteen, perhaps.”

“Perhaps?”

“I stopped counting after the first one broke my nose.”

“You should keep a notebook.”

“I have one.”

He produces a small black book from inside his coat. The cover is stained with oil. When he opens it, pages of figures and drawings spill across his knees.

The sketches are not foolish. They show curved spars, lines of force, notes about canvas and rope. One page has a bird drawn from below, every feather marked.

“You drew that?”

“The bird did not volunteer.”

“Oliver.”

“Yes.”

He shuts the notebook before you can see more.

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

The landlord's son comes into the alley carrying a broom. He sees the frame and kicks one of the slats.

“Move your rubbish,” he says.

Oliver catches the frame before it falls. “It is not rubbish.”

“Everything you own is rubbish.”

The boy raises the broom. You know him by sight. He is seventeen, broad, and fond of finding reasons to use his size.

“That will break,” Oliver says.

“Good.”

You put your hand on the window latch. The boy looks up.

“Leave it,” you say.

He laughs. “You paying for the pavement?”

“No. I am paying to watch you make a fool of yourself. Do it without damaging the frame.”

The landlord's son looks from you to Oliver. Oliver has gone very still.

“You should keep your tongue inside,” the boy says.

“You should keep your broom away from other people's work.”

The boy mutters and moves on. Oliver waits until the kitchen door closes.

“You know,” he says, “some people would call that courage.”

“Some people would be mistaken.”

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

After the boy is gone, Oliver checks every joint. His thumb presses each knot as though listening for a fault beneath the cloth.

“You did not have to say anything,” he tells you.

“I know.”

“He might complain.”

“He complains when the weather changes.”

“He might complain about me.”

“He does that when the weather stays the same.”

Oliver laughs, but his eyes remain on the frame.

You have learned that he laughs when a room grows too quiet. He laughs after constables pass. He laughs when his stomach makes a sound loud enough to hear through the glass.

“What is it meant to do?” you ask.

His fingers stop.

“It is meant to leave the ground.”

The alley seems to shrink around the words. A bucket tips somewhere above you. Water threads down the brick wall.

“A balloon?” you ask.

“No.”

“A kite?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

He looks up. “A person.”

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

You close the window halfway. Not because of the cold. The room feels suddenly exposed.

“People do not fly.”

“I am aware.”

“Birds do.”

“I have been studying them.”

“Birds have wings.”

“So will I.”

He says it with the calm of a boy telling you he plans to mend a boot. There is no performance in his face. He has not saved the dramatic part for you.

“You mean to make wings.”

“I mean to make a glider.”

“That is a word for a thing people have not seen.”

“It is a word for the thing before people see it.”

You turn the chair away from the window, then back again. The movement takes three attempts because the table catches one wheel.

Oliver watches without reaching for you.

“How high?” you ask.

“Above the roofs first.”

“And then?”

“Above the clouds.”

The answer is quieter than the others.

## [BEAT 6 — choice]

Oliver places both hands on the frame. “You are the first person I have told.”

You look at the patchwork blanket, the stolen wire, the rough marks on his knuckles. Above him, a gull turns in a circle without moving its wings.

You say:

- “Then I believe you. Do not make me regret it.”
- “You are going to get yourself killed.”
- “I want to see what is above the clouds.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

Whatever answer you give, Oliver does not celebrate. He absorbs it, as if placing a small new part into the machine he is building.

“No one else listens,” he says.

“Most people have sense.”

“That is what they call it.”

He lifts the frame and carries it toward the abandoned washhouse at the end of the alley. The door has been off its hinges for years. Inside, weeds grow through the cracked floor.

“This is where you build it?”

“This is where I hide it.”

“From the landlord's son?”

“From everyone.”

You wheel as close to the washhouse as the broken stones allow. Your chair jolts hard enough to send pain up your leg. Oliver turns at once, but you raise a hand.

“Do not fuss.”

“I was not.”

“You were thinking about it.”

He nods toward the doorway. “There is a smoother path around the back.”

“Then show me.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

The path behind the washhouse runs beside a wall furred with moss. Oliver clears loose bricks while you guide the chair through the gaps. Twice, the wheel drops into mud. Each time he steadies the handle only when you ask.

The hidden room smells of wet plaster and rust. Oliver sets the frame beneath a shelf.

On the shelf are dozens of pieces. Thin ribs. A bent pulley. A strip of silk. A brass hinge with teeth like a tiny comb.

You reach toward the hinge.

“Careful,” he says.

“Is it dangerous?”

“Only to fingers.”

You pull back.

He crouches beside you. “I will make it work.”

“You have not asked whether I think you should.”

“I know what you think.”

“Do you?”

He looks toward the high, broken window where a patch of sky shows through.

“You think nobody owns it,” he says.

You do not answer. He smiles as though silence is close enough.
