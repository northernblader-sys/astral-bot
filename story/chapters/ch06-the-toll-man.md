# Chapter 6: The Toll Man

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

The man in the brown coat waits at the end of the alley every morning for a week.

He is not a constable. Constables wear blue. This man carries a leather ledger and a brass-tipped cane. He lets the tip hover above the stones.

Oliver calls him the Toll Man.

“What toll?” you ask.

“Whatever he thinks I owe.”

“For walking?”

“For being noticed.”

The man watches Oliver bring willow strips into the washhouse, leave with empty pockets, and return with wire. At noon, he writes in the ledger.

You roll your chair nearer the window. The left wheel gives a faint click. Oliver hears it.

“Do not come outside,” he says.

“That is not outside.”

Oliver smiles without looking up.

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

On the eighth morning, the Toll Man enters the washhouse.

He ducks beneath the lintel, though the doorway is tall enough. His coat smells of wet wool and tobacco.

“Oliver Wright,” he says.

Oliver keeps working on the cradle. “That is what they call me.”

“You have been taking material from the machine yard.”

“The machine yard has material.”

“You have been seen.”

“That is unlucky.”

The man's gaze moves across the ribs, the spars, and the canvas.

“And you are?”

“The person who was here first.”

His mouth tightens. “I am asking for your name.”

“You heard me.”

Oliver puts down the pliers. The washhouse is very quiet. Even the gulls have stopped arguing on the roof.

The Toll Man opens his ledger. “The yard has reported missing brass, linen, copper, and tools.”

“Then the yard should count more carefully,” Oliver says.

“You think jokes will help.”

“No. I think they are free.”

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

The man taps the ledger with one finger. “There is a fee for unauthorized use of this district's materials.”

“A fee?” you ask.

“A restitution charge.”

“You are not the yard.”

“I collect on behalf of people who prefer not to come into alleys.”

“How convenient for them.”

He looks at your chair, then at the bandage beneath your blanket. “You have a sharp tongue for someone who cannot stand.”

Oliver moves before you can answer. Not toward the man. Between him and the workbench.

“Do not talk to her like that.”

The Toll Man smiles. “There it is.”

“There what is?”

“The reason I have found you so quickly.”

He reaches for the canvas. Oliver catches his wrist.

The man's cane strikes the floor. Not hard. Once.

Outside, a constable turns the corner.

Oliver lets go.

## [BEAT 4 — plea]

The constable comes through the door with a hand on his club. The Toll Man steps aside, leaving Oliver exposed.

“This boy has stolen from the machine yard,” he says.

“Did you see him?” the constable asks you.

Oliver's shoulders have gone rigid. The frame stands behind him, all its ribs and joints visible. If the constable searches the room, the work is finished. If he takes Oliver, the work is finished. Your hands tighten around the chair's armrest.

“I saw him take nothing,” you say.

The Toll Man opens his ledger. “You were not present.”

“I am present now.”

“You expect the constable to believe a crippled girl over a written report?”

You look at the constable's face. His eyes keep returning to the machine.

“I expect him to read the report,” you say. “If it names a tool, ask which tool. If it names a witness, ask where the witness stands. If it names a boy, ask why the collector came before the law.”

The constable looks at the ledger.

The Toll Man closes it.

[Stakes: real plea. Success keeps the frame in the washhouse and sends the enforcer away for now. Failure allows the frame to be confiscated or damaged, with no physical harm to Oliver.]

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

The constable asks the Toll Man to show the report. The man turns two pages, slowly.

“The yard's owner does not require a formal complaint for recovered property.”

“Recovered from where?” you ask.

“From the boy.”

“Then search him.”

Oliver looks at you. You do not look back.

The constable searches his coat. He finds a pencil stub, a button, bread, and blue cloth. No brass. No tools.

The Toll Man's face remains calm.

“The property is hidden,” he says.

“In my room?” you ask.

“It may be.”

“Then search it.”

He points to the frame. “That structure is evidence.”

The constable reaches for the leading spar.

Oliver says, “Do not.”

The word comes out rough. The constable's hand stops. For a breath, all three men look at him as if he has become larger.

You speak before the moment turns.

“If it breaks, you will have damaged evidence before proving it belongs to anyone.”

The constable withdraws his hand.

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

The Toll Man steps toward you. “You are making a mistake.”

“I make several each day. You will have to be specific.”

“You are attaching yourself to a thief.”

“I am attached to a chair.”

His eyes narrow.

“The boy has no property,” he says. “That frame can be taken under district ordinance.”

“Show me the ordinance.”

“You know what I mean.”

“I know what you want.”

The constable looks between you. “Is there an ordinance?”

The Toll Man does not answer.

Oliver's hand closes around the edge of the cradle. He is ready to grab the frame and run, though there is nowhere to run with a glider frame twice his size.

You address the constable. “If you are here as law, take him for a proven charge. If you are here to carry furniture for a man with a ledger, say so plainly.”

The Toll Man turns his cane toward the door. “This is not finished.”

“It never is with men who keep ledgers.”

He leaves.

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

The constable lingers, looking at the scraps, the frame, and Oliver's split lip.

“You should not build that,” he says.

“Why?” Oliver asks.

“Because it will not work.”

“Have you tried?”

The constable almost smiles. “Just keep out of the machine yard.”

“Does that apply to the Toll Man?”

The smile disappears.

He leaves without answering.

Oliver waits until the constable's boots fade. Then he lifts the canvas. A strip along the trailing edge has been cut.

The knife mark is clean.

“He did that when he came in,” Oliver says.

“Can you mend it?”

“Yes.”

“Then mend it.”

Oliver sits on the floor. He threads a needle. His hands shake once, then settle.

“You should not have done that,” he says.

“I know.”

“He will come back.”

“I know.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

By nightfall, the cut is sewn with thread from the lining of Oliver's coat.

“You will freeze,” you say.

“I have a blanket.”

“The blanket belongs to the washhouse.”

“It has not complained.”

You reach into the pocket sewn beneath your chair's cushion and take out three coins. They are meant for medicine. You place them on the bench.

Oliver stares at them. “No.”

“I did not ask you to take them.”

“You need them.”

“I need the frame to stay in one piece.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is tonight.”

He pushes the coins back. You push them toward him. Neither of you speaks until the last light leaves the window.

At last he takes one coin.

“A loan,” he says.

“A bribe.”

“For whom?”

“The future.”

He closes his fingers around it. Outside, the brown coat is gone from the alley. The empty place it leaves feels temporary.
