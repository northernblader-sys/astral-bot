# Chapter 5: Names We Don't Ask For

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

The sky is clear for three days, and Oliver spends all three under the washhouse roof. He has made a bench from a packing case. One leg is shorter than the others.

He has been measuring everything.

The spar. The cradle. The distance between the wall and the workbench. He measures the window, the washhouse door, the length of your chair's armrest.

On the fourth morning, he takes a length of string and circles your head with it.

“What are you doing?”

“Testing a theory.”

“Your theory has hands.”

He marks the string with charcoal. “Hold still.”

“I am holding still.”

“You are glaring.”

“That is not a movement.”

“It shifts the measurement.”

He lets go. The string falls around his wrist.

“What is it for?”

“A later problem.”

“You are building something for my head?”

“I am building something for a problem near your head.”

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

You refuse to speak to him for the next seven minutes. Oliver lasts four.

“Are you angry?”

“No.”

“That answer sounded angry.”

“You have a poor ear.”

“I have two.”

He returns to sanding a narrow brass plate. The filing makes a small, steady rasp. Outside, a coal cart passes, the horse's hooves slipping on the wet stones.

“You could tell me,” you say.

“I could.”

“You have decided not to.”

“For now.”

“Why?”

“Because if I tell you, you will ask whether it works.”

“Does it?”

“There.”

You turn your chair away from the bench. The left wheel bumps the wall.

Oliver's voice softens. “It might.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only honest one.”

You look over your shoulder. He has set the brass plate beside the locked box. The two tiny hinges catch the light.

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

At noon, you ask him a question he has avoided by being busy.

“Where did you sleep before the washhouse?”

He fits a pin into the frame. “Here and there.”

“That is not a place.”

“It is several.”

“Do they have roofs?”

“Some.”

“Beds?”

“Occasionally.”

“People?”

Oliver gives the pin a tap. “Often.”

You wait. He knows you are waiting. The silence stretches until a fly lands on the canvas and walks across the fish-shaped patch.

“My mother worked at the thread mill,” he says. “My father repaired boilers when anyone paid him. Then the mill closed its upper floor.”

“What happened?”

“The roof came down.”

He does not say whether they were beneath it.

“Both of them?” you ask.

“That is how families become stories.”

He turns the frame over. A knot has loosened beneath the spar.

You do not ask the question that would make him say where the bodies went.

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

“Did you have a different name then?” Oliver asks.

“No.”

“Everyone has a different name somewhere.”

“Not everyone.”

He ties the knot again. “Your aunt calls you Mon.”

“She calls me that when she remembers I exist.”

“Is that why you sit at the window?”

“I sit at the window because the table is too low.”

“That is a practical answer.”

“You asked a practical question.”

Oliver leans back against the bench. “Do you know why people call me Wright?”

“Because it is your name.”

“Because my father was a wright. Woodwork, mostly. He could make a wheel from a tree and have it turning by supper.”

He looks at the glider frame.

“He said anything with a joint could be taught to move.”

“Did he teach you?”

“He tried.”

The answer lands without explanation. You pull your chair nearer.

## [BEAT 5 — choice]

He asks about your leg while the light is still good. Not with pity. He asks because the bandage has slipped and because he has noticed you gripping the armrest.

“How did it happen?” he says.

You can answer:

- “A carriage overturned. I was inside it.”
- “The doctor says it is none of your business.”
- “I do not know how to tell it without making the room smaller.”

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

Oliver accepts your answer without trying to improve it.

He only asks, “Does it hurt now?”

“Yes.”

“Does it always?”

“No.”

“That is something.”

“You are very generous with small things.”

“Small things are what I can afford.”

He takes the end of the bandage and points to the frayed edge. “May I?”

You nod. He reties it without tightening the cloth. His fingers are warm from the brass plate. He does not stare at the scar. He watches the knot.

“Can you walk?” he asks.

“Not freely.”

He considers this. “Can you stand?”

“For a little while.”

“Can you climb?”

“If the building catches fire, I will become very persuasive.”

His mouth tilts.

“You do not have to answer every question,” he says.

“You have spent weeks answering none.”

“That is different.”

“It is the same sport.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

Later, you find the name scratched into the underside of his bench.

OLIVER WRIGHT.

The letters are deep, made by a nail rather than a pen. Beneath them, almost hidden by the shorter leg, another line has been carved.

DO NOT ASK FOR THE REST.

You call him over.

“Did you write this?”

He peers beneath the bench. “No.”

“You know who did?”

“Perhaps.”

“You are becoming irritating.”

“That is hereditary.”

He sits on the floor to examine the letters. The glider's ribs cast shadows over his shoulders.

“When I was little, people used to ask where my mother was,” he says. “They did not want the answer. They wanted to decide what sort of boy I was.”

You hold the string he used around your head.

“What sort of boy are you?”

“The sort who measures before cutting.”

“That is not a character.”

“It is when you are poor.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

At dusk, Oliver shuts the washhouse door and checks the alley through a crack. The constables have begun walking in pairs near the foundry road. Their boots leave clean marks in the mud.

He returns to the bench and opens the locked box. Inside, the brass plate has been joined to a strip of leather. The tiny hinges flex when he presses them.

You say nothing.

He closes the box.

“You may ask,” he says.

“I may?”

“You may ask what it is.”

“What is it?”

“A promise.”

“That is worse than a practical answer.”

He holds the box against his chest. “It will be finished when it is ready.”

“And if it is dreadful?”

“Then you may tell me.”

“I will.”

“I know.”

He says your name before leaving. “Good night, Monica.”

You have heard it hundreds of times from people who needed you to move, wait, eat, listen, or forgive. In Oliver's mouth it sounds like a place he intends to return to.
