# Chapter 1: The Boy Who Watched Birds

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

The window sticks in damp weather. You brace one hand against the sill and lean your shoulder into the frame before it gives. Your chair will not fit close enough by itself, so you use the brass latch as a handhold. The bandage around your leg has gone stiff at the edges.

Below, the alley is a narrow strip of brick, soot, and yesterday's rain. A pair of chimney swifts cuts over it, black as ink against the pale morning. They vanish above the roofs before the factory whistle begins.

The boy is already there.

Oliver Wright crouches beside a crate with a screwdriver between his teeth. A bird has landed on the crooked chimney opposite him. Oliver watches it without moving, except for one finger tapping against his knee.

You rap the window.

He looks up. “Morning, window ghost.”

“Morning, alley rat.”

He grins and returns to the crate. The smile is quick, gone before you can decide whether it was meant for you or for the bird.

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

The building's kitchen maid brings your breakfast in a chipped bowl and leaves it on the table beside the window. You pull the tray close with a length of cord tied to its handle. By the time you have managed the spoon, Oliver has moved three paces along the alley.

He is wearing a coat too large for him, with the sleeves rolled twice. Brass screws glint from one pocket. A leather strap runs around his head and holds a pair of cracked lenses over his eyes.

“Those yours?” you call.

“Borrowed.”

“From whom?”

“A gentleman who was careless enough to leave them unattended.”

“That means stolen.”

“Only if he notices.”

He lifts the lenses toward the chimney swift. The bird turns, rises, and slips between two rooflines.

“What are you looking for?” you ask.

Oliver takes the goggles off. “Evidence.”

“Of what?”

He points upward, though there is nothing there now but clouds. “That they are doing it properly.”

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

You have watched him for six mornings. He gathers wire from rubbish behind the machinists' row and trades buttons for canvas. Once, he spent half an hour beneath a baker's cart, studying the wheels while the driver shouted at him to move.

He never asks what you are doing at the window. No one does. The landlady says it is good that you have a place where you can sit quietly. Your aunt says the same, though she has not visited since the winter.

Oliver points at your chair. “Is that wheel meant to wobble?”

“It is meant to turn.”

“It turns with conviction.”

“That is more than can be said for your coat.”

He comes to the building's wall and looks up at you. The alley makes his face appear longer and younger at once.

“You could oil the wheel,” he says.

“You could mind your own business.”

“I am. You are in it.”

The answer catches you before you can throw one back.

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

A cart rattles at the mouth of the alley. Oliver slips behind the rain barrel. Two constables pass without looking down.

When they are gone, Oliver emerges with a length of copper wire looped around his wrist.

“Did you see the one with the packet?” he asks.

“I saw both.”

“He had crumbs on his glove. That is how you know a man has never had to steal his breakfast.”

You lower the spoon. “You steal yours?”

“Sometimes breakfast steals itself.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the one I have.”

Oliver looks up again. His eyes follow a gull climbing above the smoke.

“They make it look easy,” he says.

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

By noon the light has moved across your room. You have read the same page of a book three times. Oliver has not returned to the alley.

You tap on the glass because there is no reason not to.

He appears below the window with a sack over one shoulder. “You missed the exciting portion.”

“Were there more birds?”

“A pigeon fell off a roof.”

“Did it survive?”

“With dignity damaged, but yes.”

He puts the sack down and takes out a piece of wood carved into a narrow curve. The grain has been rubbed smooth. On one side, a row of holes has been drilled at careful intervals.

“What is that?” you ask.

“A rib.”

“You have a broken bird in the sack?”

“You have a cruel mind.”

“I have a window.”

He holds the carved piece up to the light. The curve is delicate, almost too thin to trust.

“A rib of what?” you ask.

Oliver looks toward the chimney, then back at you. “Something that has not been named yet.”

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

The next morning he brings a tin cup of tea and sets it on the ledge. It is too far for you to reach without leaning out, so he climbs onto the drainpipe and pushes it nearer with one finger.

“If you fall,” you say, “I will tell everyone you were killed by tea.”

“Make sure you mention the heroic rescue attempt.”

“There will not be one.”

He settles on the sill below yours, boots on a narrow brick projection. You can see the torn seam in his glove. Beneath it, his knuckles are scraped and blackened.

“You should wash your hands,” you say.

“I did.”

“With what?”

“Rain.”

“Rain is not soap.”

“It is free.”

He drinks from the cup he kept for himself. The tea is weak enough to show the metal at the bottom.

For a while neither of you speaks. A sparrow lands on the gutter. Oliver watches it, and you watch him watching it.

“You are allowed to blink,” you say.

“I am saving them.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

That evening, the sky clears enough for the first stars to appear above the roofs. Your chair is turned toward the open window, one wheel resting against the table leg so it will not drift.

Oliver is below, fitting wire through the carved rib. He works by the yellow light from a stolen lantern.

“Oliver.”

He glances up.

“What happens if it breaks?”

“The rib?”

“The thing you have not named.”

He studies the wire between his fingers. “Then I find out why.”

“And if you cannot?”

“Then I find someone who can.”

“You always answer like that?”

“Only when people ask sensible questions.”

The lantern flame shakes. A gust moves through the alley and lifts the loose edge of his coat.

You think of telling him that the roof is high enough, that the birds have hollow bones and years of practice, that wanting something does not make it possible. Instead you say, “The wire is crooked.”

He looks at it.

“You are right,” he says. “Hold the lantern.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

He climbs until he can pass the lantern through the window. You keep hold of the handle while he reaches down for a tool. His shoulder brushes the side of your chair. He does not ask whether you can move back. He simply finds the space that exists and works inside it.

The wire straightens under his pliers.

“There,” he says.

“That is still crooked.”

“It is a different kind of crooked.”

You look past him. The swifts have gone. Above the chimneys, the stars sit in the clean dark, unreachable and unconcerned.

Oliver follows your gaze.

“Tomorrow,” he says, “I will show you something.”

“A stolen object?”

“A proper invention.”

“You have never shown me an improper one.”

He smiles. This time the smile stays.

You close the window only after he has climbed down. The latch catches on the first try. In the quiet room, the brass wheel of your chair gives a small, answering click as you turn toward the table.
