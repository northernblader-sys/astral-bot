 
# Chapter 3: Scrap and Silence

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

The first sign of trouble is a missing spoon.

You find it beneath the table, bent nearly in half. The cook is shouting in the corridor.

“Three days running. Three days, Mrs. Vale. He thinks because the boy is small, nobody sees him.”

Oliver sits on the floor beside your chair, trying to fit a brass washer into the hinge of a wing frame. He has brought the piece through the window in his sleeve.

“What did you take?” you ask.

“Nothing.”

“The spoon is confessing.”

“The spoon was already bent.”

“It was not.”

He turns the washer between his fingers. “The washer was free.”

“There is no free brass.”

“There is free brass if a person is not looking after it.”

The shouting comes closer.

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

You point to the space beneath the bed.

Oliver shakes his head. “Too obvious.”

“You are covered in coal dust.”

“That is camouflage.”

“You are standing in a white room.”

He glances down at himself. “Excellent camouflage for a ghost.”

The door handle moves. You pull the cord tied to the tray and drag your chair backward. Oliver slips behind the curtain beside the window. The frame goes under your blanket.

Mrs. Vale enters with a basket against her hip. She is a square woman with a red mark across one cheek where the kitchen steam has burned her.

“Has the boy been here?”

“Which boy?”

She looks at you. “Do not play clever.”

“I am not playing. I am asking for rules.”

Her eyes go to the missing spoon. “He took from the kitchen.”

“Then search the kitchen.”

“I am asking whether he has been in this room.”

Behind the curtain, Oliver makes the smallest possible sound. It might be a cough. It might be a laugh.

## [BEAT 3 — plea]

Mrs. Vale comes farther inside. The room is too small for her and your chair and the concealed frame. Her gaze settles on the blanket over your knees.

“What is under there?”

You place both hands on the blanket. “My leg.”

Her face changes. Not pity. Calculation.

“Do not be disgusting.”

“You asked.”

“The boy steals because people let him.”

“Perhaps people should lock their spoons.”

She takes one step toward you. Oliver is silent now.

You make yourself look bored. “If you found him, what would you do?”

“Send for the constable.”

“For a spoon?”

“For a pattern.”

You look at the basket on her hip. A crust of bread rests under a cloth. The kitchen's morning loaf is still warm enough to smell.

“Mrs. Vale,” you say, “I took it.”

Her eyebrows rise.

“The spoon. I used it to reach something beneath the scuttle. The brass washer was mine already. I dropped the spoon because the chair caught on the rug.”

It is a good lie, except for the part where it makes you smaller.

[Stakes: scripted outcome. Monica's lie protects Oliver, but it costs her the comfort of being truthful with Mrs. Vale.]

## [BEAT 4 — dialogue]

Mrs. Vale studies your leg through the blanket. The lie sits between you, ugly and warm.

“You expect me to believe that?”

“No.”

“Then why say it?”

“Because if you send for a constable, he will ask why a kitchen spoon was in my room. That will take time. You have bread in your basket, and I suspect you are late.”

She looks down at the basket.

Oliver's boot nudges the curtain. You cannot see him, but you feel the movement in the floorboards.

Mrs. Vale sighs. “The boy is not worth a beating.”

“I did not say he was.”

“You said nothing.”

“That is often the difficulty.”

She takes the spoon from beneath the table. “Return it.”

“It is bent.”

“Then return it bent.”

She leaves. The door shuts with a soft click.

Oliver comes out from behind the curtain, eyes wide.

“You took the blame.”

“I took a spoon.”

“You said it was yours.”

“It was under my blanket.”

“That is not how ownership works.”

“It is how rooms work.”

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

He kneels and removes the frame from beneath the blanket. One of the ribs has cracked.

“What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“The rib is split.”

“It was a weak rib.”

“You made it.”

“That explains the weakness.”

You notice the black smear on his wrist. He has been into the machine yard again. The yard has a wall topped with iron spikes and a watchman who likes to throw stones.

“You cannot keep stealing from the foundry,” you say.

“I do not keep stealing.”

“You do it repeatedly.”

“That is what keeping means?”

He sets the cracked rib beside the unbroken one. The two pieces are nearly identical.

“If I stop,” he says, “the work stops.”

“Then let it stop for a day.”

“You would not say that about your chair.”

The room goes quiet.

Oliver looks at you, startled by his own words. He reaches for the hinge, then withdraws his hand.

“I did not mean…”

“You meant it.”

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

You move the chair toward the window. The rear wheel catches on the rug. You pull harder. Pain flashes from your ankle to your hip.

Oliver rises.

“Do not touch it.”

He stops.

You get the wheel free yourself. The rug bunches beneath the tire. For a second the chair tilts, and the sill seems very far away.

“There,” you say.

Oliver's hands remain at his sides. “You could have asked.”

“I could have.”

Outside, the alley is empty. A scrap of newspaper tumbles along the gutter.

Oliver retrieves the cracked rib.

“I am sorry,” he says.

“That is not useful.”

“I know.”

“Then make a new rib.”

He nods.

The apology stays in the room even after he returns to the washhouse.

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

Near dusk, Oliver brings back the spoon. He has flattened it against a stone.

“Mrs. Vale asked for this.”

“It is worse.”

“It has character.”

You take it and set it beside your bowl.

He studies the chair's rear wheel. “The axle is loose.”

“I know.”

“I can fix it.”

“With stolen brass?”

“With a bolt from the road.”

“Roads have bolts?”

“The city is full of generous objects.”

You should send him away. Instead, you hold the chair steady while he tightens the nut with a little wrench. His fingers move carefully, never brushing your bandage.

“There,” he says.

You roll forward. The wheel turns without its old wobble.

“That is better,” you admit.

“You are welcome.”

“Do not become proud.”

“Too late.”

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

He leaves after dark. From the window, you watch him cross the alley with the cracked rib under his coat. At the wall, he pauses and looks up.

“What?” you call.

“Nothing.”

“You are standing there.”

“I am considering the best route.”

“The route is straight.”

“That is why it is suspicious.”

He disappears around the corner.

You take the flattened spoon from the table. Its handle is marked with a tiny line where the stone struck it. The lie has not made you noble. It has made the room feel different. Mrs. Vale will remember it. You will remember that she knew.

Outside, a whistle sounds from the foundry road. Another answers from farther away.

For the first time, Oliver does not come back before morning.
