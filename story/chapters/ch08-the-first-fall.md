# Chapter 8: The First Fall

## [BEAT 1 — dialogue]

Oliver chooses the abandoned quarry for the first public attempt because the quarry has a hill, a long clear slope, and no roof to catch the wing.

It also has a crowd by noon.

Someone has told the factory boys. They stand along the ridge with their caps tilted back, ready to laugh before anything happens. A pair of women from the laundry watch from behind a fence. The Toll Man is nowhere in sight, which does not make the place safer.

You sit at the top of the slope with your chair braced against a sack of gravel. Oliver checks the knots.

“You can still stop,” you say.

“I could.”

“That was not permission.”

“I know.”

He wears the leather belt across his chest. The cradle is tied to the glider's center. His goggles are gone. He has made room for a strip of cloth to cover his eyes if the wind becomes too strong.

Below, a boy shouts, “Fly, orphan!”

Oliver bows to the crowd.

## [BEAT 2 — dialogue]

The glider is dragged to the top of the slope. It takes four boys and Oliver to move it. None of the four boys admit they helped. They put their hands to the frame when nobody is looking, then step away and pretend the hill did the work.

You hold the launch rope.

Oliver kneels beside your chair. “When I say pull, pull straight.”

“I know how rope works.”

“You know how to insult rope.”

“It is an underrated skill.”

His hand rests on the wheel of your chair. He does not turn it. He only steadies himself.

“If the wing tilts left,” he says, “let go.”

“If it tilts right?”

“Let go.”

“If it tilts upward?”

“Do not let go.”

“You have made the safest instruction very confusing.”

He looks down the slope. Grass bends in a clean line toward the quarry floor.

“The wind is good,” he says.

You can feel it on your face. It smells of chalk and wet iron.

## [BEAT 3 — dialogue]

Oliver climbs into the cradle. The frame settles around him. The wing rises on both sides, tall enough to hide the crowd.

For a second he is still.

Then the factory boys begin to chant.

“Mad boy. Mad boy. Mad boy.”

Oliver raises one hand.

You pull the rope.

The glider moves. At first it drags. The skids bite into the grass. Then the wind catches the canvas, and the whole frame surges forward.

Oliver shouts something you cannot hear.

The left wing lifts. The right follows. The skids leave the ground.

The crowd falls silent.

The glider climbs no higher than a man's waist, but it moves through the air for six full seconds. The canvas snaps. The frame shudders. Oliver's head turns toward the clouds.

Then the right spar folds.

The wing drops.

## [BEAT 4 — battle]

The glider strikes the slope and breaks into a rain of wood, brass, and canvas. Oliver is thrown from the cradle. He rolls once, catches on the edge of a drainage trench, and disappears below the grass.

You push the chair forward. The gravel sack tears loose behind you. The chair gathers speed.

The slope is too steep. Your hands slip from the rims. You grab the crutch across your lap and jam it against the ground. The chair veers toward the trench.

Oliver is below. One arm is caught beneath the spar. The broken wing shifts each time he tries to breathe.

You have no reach. No strength enough to pull him free. The crowd is running downhill, but the frame can slide before they arrive.

You hook the launch rope around the chair's axle and shout for the nearest boy to take it. He does not move.

“Take the rope!”

The boy stares.

“If you can laugh, you can pull!”

Three hands seize the line. Together you lean back. The rope tightens. Oliver's trapped arm comes free as the spar drags against the grass.

[Fixed encounter: The Fall. The danger is the collapsing glider and the slope, not a human enemy. The sequence must resolve with Oliver alive, but the frame is badly damaged.]

## [BEAT 5 — dialogue]

Oliver crawls clear. Blood runs from his temple into one eyebrow. He tries to stand and falls onto one knee.

You stop beside him, breathless from shouting.

“Do not move,” you say.

“I am not.”

“You are attempting to.”

“That is different.”

The four factory boys pull the broken spar away. One of them has cut his palm. He stares at the blood as if it has betrayed him.

Oliver looks toward the crowd. They have found their voices again. Some laugh. Some whisper. One woman crosses herself.

“How long?” Oliver asks.

“What?”

“How long was it up?”

You glance at the torn cloth, the crushed skids, the hill. “Six seconds.”

His eyes close.

“Six is not nothing,” you say.

“It is less than seven.”

“You have not earned seven yet.”

He opens one eye. “That sounds like a challenge.”

“It is a warning.”

## [BEAT 6 — dialogue]

The Toll Man appears at the ridge after the crowd begins to leave. He has chosen a clean place to stand, away from the broken frame.

“You have made a spectacle,” he says.

Oliver presses a hand to his temple. “People enjoy those.”

“The quarry owner will charge for the damage.”

“The quarry owner did not own the hill.”

“He owns the risk.”

You turn your chair so the Toll Man must address you from the front.

“The frame was on the grass,” you say.

“And now it is in pieces.”

“That is the usual result of building something.”

“You encourage him.”

“You frighten him.”

The man's gaze drops to your bandage, then returns to your face. “You cannot protect him from what he is.”

“Neither can you.”

Oliver reaches for the broken spar. You catch his sleeve.

“Do not,” you say.

He lets go. The Toll Man watches the gesture.

“This will end badly,” he says.

“Then you will have to wait and see.”

## [BEAT 7 — dialogue]

The return journey takes an hour. Oliver carries the smallest pieces. The largest are tied to the back of your chair, where they knock against the spokes.

At the quarry gate, the boy with the cut palm approaches.

“It went up,” he says.

Oliver looks at him.

“Before it broke,” the boy adds.

“I know.”

“I saw.”

Oliver gives him the brass hinge that survived the fall.

The boy stares at it. “What is this?”

“A piece of the next one.”

“You need it.”

“I need better witnesses.”

The boy closes his hand around the hinge.

On the road, Oliver's steps begin to drag. He refuses the chair when you offer it. You cannot carry him. You cannot make him accept the limits of his own body.

You can slow the chair and make him walk beside you.

So you do.

## [BEAT 8 — dialogue]

In the washhouse, Oliver lays the broken frame across the floor. The ribs are split. The cradle has one missing brace. The canvas is torn from end to end.

He stands over it until the lantern burns low.

“You can stop,” you say.

“No.”

“That was not a command.”

“It sounded like one.”

He kneels and picks up the folded piece of wing. Blood from his temple drops onto the cloth.

“You nearly died,” you say.

“Nearly is a distance.”

“A small one.”

He begins untying the knots. His hands are clumsy now.

You take the needle from the bench and put it in his palm.

“You cannot sew with your eyes closed.”

“I can try.”

“Try after you clean the blood.”

He looks at you. There is no joke ready.

You hold out a cloth.

He takes it.

The first thing they will repair is not the wing. It is the boy.
